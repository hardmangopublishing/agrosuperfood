# Building a Dried Pantry You Actually Cook From

There are two kinds of dried pantry. One is a shelf of well made jars that gets photographed and slowly discarded. The other is a working set of ingredients that runs down and gets replaced.

The difference has almost nothing to do with the technique covered on the [drying hub](hub-drying.html) and almost everything to do with what you chose to dry, in what portions, and where you put it.

!!fig img-drying-variables

## Dry what you already cook with

This is the whole principle and it is regularly ignored in favour of what is interesting.

Dried mango is delightful and will sit in the cupboard. Dried onion goes into a very large share of everything you make. The second is worth ten times the first even though the first is more fun to produce.

Look at what you actually cooked last week and dry that. For most households the answer is onion, garlic, mushroom, tomato, pepper, carrot, celery, and a few herbs. Those eight jars will do more work than twenty jars of unusual fruit.

The exception is snacking. If dried apple rings genuinely get eaten as snacks in your house, they belong in the pantry too, because getting eaten is the only test that matters.

## The core eight

If you build nothing else, build these.

**Onion.** Sliced or diced. The most used jar in any dried pantry. Goes into everything, needs no blanching, and rehydrates in minutes.

**Garlic.** Sliced thin, or dried and ground into powder. Home made garlic powder is noticeably better than most commercial versions.

**Mushroom.** Sliced, plus a small jar of powder. The powder is the most useful single item in this hub.

**Tomato.** Halved cherry tomatoes and a jar of powder. The powder replaces tomato paste and does not go off in the refrigerator.

**Carrot.** Blanched, sliced thin. Part of the soup base and useful alone.

**Celery.** Blanched, sliced. Less useful alone, essential in a base.

**Pepper.** Strips. Adds colour and sweetness to anything slow cooked.

**Herbs.** Whatever you grow. Oregano and thyme first.

That set costs a handful of afternoons and covers most of what a kitchen needs from this category.

## The soup base jar

This is the single item that turns a dried pantry from a store into a habit.

Dry onion, carrot, celery, and leek separately, since they take different times, then combine them in one jar in roughly equal parts by volume. Add a spoonful of dried garlic and a good pinch of dried herbs.

A handful of that jar plus water and a stock cube becomes a soup base, a stew foundation, a braise, or the start of a risotto, with no chopping and no planning. It is the jar that gets opened, and everything else in the pantry benefits from sitting next to something you reach for weekly.

Make it small enough that it turns over in two months. A jar you finish is worth more than a jar you preserve, and flavour declines steadily whatever you do.

Variations worth having: a mushroom-heavy version for risotto, and a tomato and pepper version for anything Mediterranean.

## Portion sizes decide use

The most common structural mistake is jars sized for storage rather than for cooking.

A large jar of dried mushroom gets rationed, because opening it feels like a decision. A small jar holding exactly enough for one risotto gets used, because using it is obvious.

Two practical rules.

**Match the jar to a meal.** If a dish takes half a cup of dried vegetables, keep jars that hold two or three of those. You will use them without thinking.

**Keep a working jar and a reserve.** A small jar in the kitchen, refilled from a larger sealed container elsewhere, with {{product:oxygen-absorbers}} in the reserve. This is covered in the [storage guide](guide-dried-storage.html), and it lets the bulk stay properly sealed while the everyday quantity stays reachable.

Large jars also age faster in use, because every opening exchanges the air inside.

## Where the pantry lives

Dried food keeps best somewhere dark and cool and gets used most somewhere visible and close to the stove. Those two facts are in tension and the resolution is a compromise rather than a rule.

The compromise that works: a labelled cupboard near where you cook, with the working jars at the front, and the reserve stock somewhere genuinely cool and dark such as a lower cupboard away from the oven or a cellar if you have one.

A dried pantry on a high shelf in another room is a dried pantry that gets forgotten. Proximity beats optimal conditions for the jars you use weekly, since those turn over before storage conditions matter much.

Label the lid as well as the side. Jars in a cupboard are seen from above.

## Cooking from it

The habit takes a few weeks to form and then becomes automatic. Three prompts help.

**Start every soup and stew from the base jar.** Once that is reflex, the pantry is working.

**Add mushroom powder to anything savoury.** Gravy, mince, tomato sauce, a stew. It disappears and improves the dish.

**Keep dried tomato in the dressing rotation.** Chopped into oil and vinegar an hour ahead, it softens and turns an ordinary dressing into something with body.

Most dried vegetables need no rehydration when a dish has liquid and time, which is the fact that makes daily use practical.

## Rotation

A pantry that only accumulates eventually gets thrown away.

**Newest at the back, oldest at the front.** The entirety of rotation, and it costs nothing.

**Review every six months.** Anything approaching a year moves to the front and gets used deliberately. Soup base jars and powders absorb surplus easily, which is what makes them useful for clearing older stock.

**Date everything.** For dried food the date usually indicates declining quality rather than a safety limit, so it tells you what to prioritise rather than what to discard.

**Do not preserve for its own sake.** A jar of apricots finished in March is a better outcome than the same jar sealed in September.

## What to dry when

The economics of this category are driven almost entirely by buying at the right moment.

**Late summer.** Tomatoes, peppers, courgettes, and herbs, all at their cheapest and best. This is the single most productive month of the year for a dehydrator.

**Autumn.** Apples, pears, plums, and mushrooms. Windfall fruit is free and dries perfectly well.

**Winter.** Citrus zest, root vegetables, and anything from the reduced shelf. A quiet season for produce and a good one for building the soup base.

**Any time.** Onions and garlic, which are cheap year round and always worth having.

**Whenever something is reduced.** Soft tomatoes, opening mushrooms, and bruised fruit are the best value inputs in this hub, because texture stops mattering once they are sliced or puréed.

## Gifts and sharing

A dried pantry produces surplus faster than most households consume it, and giving it away is a better outcome than letting it age on a shelf.

**Herb blends** are the easiest gift and the most appreciated. A small jar of an Italian or poultry blend, labelled with what is in it, costs almost nothing and is noticeably better than a supermarket equivalent.

**Soup base jars** work well for anyone who cooks. Include a short note on the ratio, since a jar of unidentified dried vegetables is less useful than one with instructions.

**Mushroom powder** in a small jar is the sleeper gift here. Most people have never used it and everyone who tries it wants more.

**Fruit leather** rolled and tied is a straightforward gift for a household with children.

**Do not give away things you would not use yourself.** The temptation is to offload the jars that failed to earn their place, and a jar of dried courgette is not a present.

Sharing also solves a real problem in this category, which is that a productive herb plant or a glut of tomatoes produces far more than one household can absorb. Drying converts the surplus into something storable, and giving it away converts it into something used.

## Run the numbers

Illustrative figures for a household of two to four, based on typical use.

| Jar | Fresh input | Yields roughly | Lasts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onion | 2 kg | 200 g | 6 to 9 months |
| Garlic | 500 g | 60 g | A year |
| Mushroom slices | 1 kg | 100 g | 6 months |
| Mushroom powder | 500 g dried slices | Small jar | 2 months in use |
| Tomato | 3 kg | 200 g | 6 months |
| Soup base blend | Mixed | 400 g jar | 2 to 3 months |
| Herbs | One plant | Small jar each | A year |

The whole core set represents perhaps six or seven machine loads spread across a season, which is a modest commitment for a pantry that covers most of a year.

## What goes wrong

**A shelf of jars nobody opens.** Dried what was interesting rather than what gets cooked. Rebuild around the core eight.

**Jars too large to use casually.** Split into a working jar and a sealed reserve.

**Forgotten because it is in another room.** Move the working jars next to the stove and accept slightly worse conditions for them.

**Everything a year old at once.** No rotation, and probably one big drying session rather than several. Spread production across the year.

**Unidentifiable contents.** No labels on the lids.

**Interesting but useless powders.** Powders age fast. Grind small batches and keep the bulk as slices.

## Do this now

1. Write down what you cooked in the past week and identify which vegetables appeared most. Dry those first.
2. Build one soup base jar from onion, carrot, celery, and leek in roughly equal parts, plus garlic and herbs.
3. Put that jar in the cupboard nearest the stove, not with the rest of the stock.
4. Use it this week, in the next soup or stew you make, before drying anything else.
