# Building a Blend Worth Keeping

A blend you assemble yourself beats a bought one on three counts: cost, freshness, and the fact that you can adjust it.

It also beats it on taste, for a reason that is structural rather than a matter of skill. Commercial blends compete on ingredient count, because a number on the front sells. A blend of forty things contains meaningful quantities of three or four and tastes muddy. A blend of four tastes of something.

The [powders hub](hub-powders.html) makes the case in outline. This page is the method.

!!fig img-powder-jars

## The four roles

Every blend worth keeping fills these, and most disappointing blends are missing one.

**A base.** The bulk ingredient, mild, providing body without dominating. Home made vegetable powder, oat flour, a plain green powder, or ground seed.

**A driver.** The thing that makes it taste of something. Mushroom for savoury, cacao for sweet, a berry powder such as {{product:acai-powder}}, or a strong spice.

**A bright note.** Acidity or aromatic lift. Citrus zest, ginger, mint, or a sour fruit powder such as {{product:camu-camu}}, which is tart enough that a small proportion does the job. This is the role most often missing, and its absence is why so many blends taste flat and heavy.

**An accent.** Small quantity, distinctive. Cinnamon, cardamom, chilli, black pepper, or a herb.

Four roles, four to five ingredients. Adding a fifth ingredient to fill a role you have already filled adds cost and subtracts clarity.

## Weigh it and write it down

The habit that separates a blend you can reproduce from a happy accident you cannot.

**Weigh in grams.** Volume measures for powders vary by density, and a tablespoon of green powder and a tablespoon of cacao are very different masses.

**Work in parts.** Four parts base, two parts driver, one part bright, one part accent, as a starting structure. Parts scale without recalculation.

**Write the ratio on the jar.** Masking tape and a pen. This costs three seconds and it is the difference between a blend you make again and one you liked once.

**Record what you changed.** If the second batch has less ginger, note it. Two or three iterations usually lands on something you keep.

**Note the date.** Blends age at the pace of their most fragile component, which is usually the greenest or most aromatic thing in the jar.

## Four blends that work

Starting points rather than recipes, expressed in parts by weight.

**The house savoury jar.** Four parts mushroom, two parts tomato, one part garlic, one part onion, plus a pinch of dried thyme. Goes into anything cooked in liquid. This is the blend most likely to be used weekly.

**Green with a lift.** Eight parts mixed green powder, one part dried citrus zest, one part ginger. The zest and ginger are what make it drinkable rather than grassy, and they are the elements commercial blends spend money adding as fruit.

**Warm sweet.** Six parts cacao, one part cinnamon, one part ginger, a small pinch of chilli and a small pinch of salt. For hot drinks, porridge, and baking.

**Soup base.** Equal parts onion, carrot, celery, and leek powder, plus one part garlic and a good pinch of mixed herbs. A heaped teaspoon plus hot water is a stock.

Each has a base, a driver, a bright note, and an accent, and none exceeds five ingredients.

## Matching a blend to a job

The most common reason a blend goes unused is that it was built for no particular purpose.

**Decide the job first.** A blend for hot drinks, a blend for savoury cooking, a blend for smoothies. One blend cannot serve all three, and trying is how you end up with something that suits none.

**Consider where it will live.** A cooking blend belongs next to the stove. A drinks blend belongs with the mugs. Storing them together means one of them is always in the wrong place.

**Consider the temperature it will meet.** Savoury blends will be heated, so they can contain things that need heat to bloom. Drink blends will be cold, so anything requiring heat is wasted in them and anything heat sensitive is safe.

**Size it to two months.** A blend degrades faster than its components stored separately, because the most fragile ingredient sets the pace. Make what you will finish.

## What to leave out

Being deliberate about exclusions improves a blend as much as the inclusions.

**Anything present in a trace.** If it is under about five percent by weight, it is contributing nothing you would notice. Either raise it or remove it.

**Duplicate roles.** Three green powders is one green powder with extra steps.

**Sweeteners, usually.** They dilute the blend and you can add sweetness at the point of use, where you have control.

**Anything you have not tasted alone.** Blending an unfamiliar powder into a batch is how a whole jar becomes unusable. Taste each component on its own first.

**Anything that clumps aggressively**, unless the blend is small and will be used quickly. Onion powder in particular can set a whole blend solid.

**Fillers.** Rice flour and similar bulk out commercial products and have no place in something you made.

## Practical mixing

**Sieve everything first, separately.** Different powders have different particle sizes and a blend of fine and coarse will separate in the jar. Sieving each component to a similar fineness keeps the blend uniform.

**Mix in a bowl, not the jar.** Shaking a jar to combine looks efficient and leaves the densest ingredient at the bottom.

**Use a whisk or a fork,** working for a full minute. Powders resist mixing more than they appear to.

**Check for streaks.** Coloured components make this easy: if you can see distinct patches of beetroot or green, keep mixing.

**Decant into a jar smaller than the batch,** so there is minimal headspace and no room for the ingredients to stratify with handling.

**Give it a day and check again.** Some blends settle and want a second mix before first use.

## Blend storage

The same rules as any powder, with one addition.

The addition is that a blend expires at the pace of its shortest lived component. A blend containing green powder is a two month blend regardless of how stable the mushroom powder in it is.

Two consequences. Make blends in small quantities. And for anything containing a fragile component, consider keeping the components separately and blending a small jar at a time, which is more work and gives noticeably better results over a year.

For robust blends made entirely of stable powders, such as a mushroom and allium savoury mix, a larger batch is reasonable.

## Blends as gifts

A jar of blend is one of the better things to give away from any of the hubs on this site, and a few things make the difference between a gift that gets used and one that sits.

**Label it properly.** Contents, ratio, and at least one suggested use. A jar of unidentified beige powder is a puzzle rather than a present, and most people will not experiment with something they cannot identify.

**Suggest the first use specifically.** A teaspoon in gravy. A teaspoon in a smoothie with banana and lemon. One concrete instruction gets a jar opened, and once opened it usually gets finished.

**Give small.** A jar someone finishes in a month is a success. A large jar that fades on their shelf is not, and blends fade faster than single powders.

**Match the blend to the person.** A savoury cooking blend for someone who cooks. A warm sweet blend for someone who does not. Giving a soup base to someone who never makes soup is a common misfire.

**Include the date.** So they know roughly when it stops being at its best.

**Mushroom salt is the reliable gift** in this category. Almost everyone finds a use for it immediately, it needs no explanation beyond the name, and it tastes of something the first time it is tried.

Sharing also solves a real problem, since a productive drying season generates far more powder than one household uses.

## Run the numbers

Illustrative structures in parts by weight.

| Blend | Base | Driver | Bright | Accent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| House savoury | Tomato 2 | Mushroom 4 | Onion 1 | Garlic 1, thyme pinch |
| Green with lift | Green 8 | Green 8 | Citrus zest 1 | Ginger 1 |
| Warm sweet | Cacao 6 | Cacao 6 | Ginger 1 | Cinnamon 1, chilli pinch |
| Soup base | Onion, carrot, celery, leek 1 each | Onion 1 | Herbs pinch | Garlic 1 |
| Mushroom salt | Salt 4 | Mushroom 1 | None | Black pepper pinch |

Two of these use the same ingredient as base and driver, which is fine when that ingredient is the point. The structure is a check rather than a rule.

## What goes wrong

**Tastes flat and heavy.** No bright note. Add citrus zest or ginger.

**Tastes muddy.** Too many ingredients. Cut to four.

**Separated in the jar.** Components at different particle sizes. Sieve each one before mixing.

**Dense ingredient at the bottom.** Mixed by shaking rather than in a bowl.

**A blend you cannot reproduce.** Ratio not written down. This is the most common and most annoying failure.

**Went dull within weeks.** Contains a fragile component. Blend smaller batches, or keep the components apart.

**Set solid.** Onion powder plus humidity. Smaller jar, desiccant, and make less at once.

## Do this now

1. Decide what job the blend is for before choosing any ingredient: cooking, drinks, or seasoning.
2. Fill four roles with four ingredients: a base, a driver, a bright note, and an accent.
3. Sieve each component separately, mix in a bowl with a whisk for a full minute, and decant into a jar with little headspace.
4. Write the ratio and the date on the lid, so a blend you liked can be made again.
