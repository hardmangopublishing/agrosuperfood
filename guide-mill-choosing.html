Impact, Stone, or Burr: Choosing a Mill

Three mechanisms cover almost every home grain mill, and the differences are practical rather than subtle. Each grinds by a different physical action, and that action determines speed, fineness, heat, and what the mill can and cannot handle.

The milling hub makes the case for milling at all. This page is the purchase.

The three mechanisms

Impact. Grain is thrown at high speed against a set of stainless steel fins or pins. The kernel shatters rather than being crushed. Very fast, very fine, and the closest home result to commercial flour texture.

Stone burr. Grain passes between two stones, one fixed and one turning. It is crushed and sheared rather than shattered. Slower, cooler, and traditional.

Steel burr. Grain passes between two metal plates with a gap you set. It is crushed. The most adjustable of the three, from cracked grain to fine flour.

Everything below follows from those three actions.

Impact mills

What they do well. Speed and fineness. Two to four minutes for enough flour for a loaf, and a texture close to shop flour, which matters if you are baking recipes written for it. They are also the most consistent batch to batch, since there is no gap to set or stones to dress.

What they cannot do. Oily or damp material. Nuts, seeds, oily grains, and anything moist will clog the chamber, and getting it out is a genuinely poor way to spend an afternoon. Check what your model is rated for.

Heat. They warm the flour noticeably. Let it cool before mixing, since warm flour accelerates fermentation unpredictably.

Noise. These are loud. Genuinely loud, closer to a vacuum cleaner than a food processor.

Buy this if you bake weekly or more, you want fine flour with no fuss, and you can tolerate a couple of minutes of noise. an electric impact mill is the standard form and the machine most home bakers end up with.

Stone burr mills

What they do well. Fine flour at lower temperature, and a texture many bakers prefer. The shearing action produces a slightly different particle profile from impact milling, which shows in the crumb.

What they cannot do. Oily material, same as impact mills. Oil clogs the pores of the stone and is difficult to remove.

Maintenance. Stones need dressing eventually, meaning the grinding surfaces are re-roughened. This is infrequent and it is a real job.

Cost. Electric stone mills are the most expensive category here by a clear margin. Manual stone mills are affordable and are genuine physical work. a manual stone mill is the manual pattern.

Buy this if you care about flour texture specifically, you are willing to spend, or you want a manual mill with no electricity requirement.

Steel burr mills

What they do well. Versatility. The gap adjusts from cracked grain for porridge through to fine flour, and steel handles oily and damp material that stones and impact mills cannot. Nuts, seeds, and coffee are all possible.

What they do less well. Very fine flour. Steel burrs generally produce a slightly coarser result than the other two at their finest setting, and a second pass helps.

Cost. The cheapest way into milling by a wide margin. a hand crank burr mill with adjustable coarseness is a hand crank version with adjustable coarseness.

Effort. Manual steel burr mills are hard work. Five hundred grams of grain is fifteen to twenty five minutes of steady cranking, which is more than most people expect.

Buy this if you are testing whether you want to mill at all, you want maximum versatility, or you specifically want a non electric option.

The honest recommendation

Start manual if you are unsure. A hand crank burr mill costs little and answers the only question that matters within a month: whether you will keep doing this. A great many electric mills sit unused because they were bought before that question was answered.

Move to electric when the cranking becomes the reason you skip a bake. That is the signal, and it is unambiguous when it arrives.

Buy electric immediately if you already bake bread weekly with shop flour. You know the habit exists; you are only changing the flour.

Do not buy a stone mill first unless you have a specific reason. They are the most expensive and the most maintenance sensitive, and their advantage is real but marginal for a beginner.

Attachments and alternatives

Stand mixer grain mill attachments. A reasonable middle path if you already own the mixer. Usually steel or stone burr, moderate speed, and no extra appliance on the counter. Check the attachment is rated for the volume you want.

Blender and food processor. These produce coarse, uneven meal rather than flour. Adequate for cracked grain and porridge, poor for bread. High powered blenders with a dedicated dry container do better and still fall short of a mill.

Spice grinders. Fine for very small quantities and they overheat quickly. Not a milling solution.

Coffee grinders. Burr coffee grinders are designed for uniform hard beans and clog on grain. Blade grinders produce uneven meal.

If you already own a high powered blender with a dry container, try it once before spending. It will show you what fresh flour tastes like even if the texture is wrong.

Specifications worth checking

Fineness adjustment. Whether it exists, and what the range is. A mill fixed at one setting is limiting.

Capacity and hopper size. How much grain it holds and how much flour it produces per run. Small hoppers mean refilling mid batch.

What it is rated for. Oily seeds, damp grain, legumes, and corn all stress mills differently. Check before rather than after.

Noise, for impact mills. Look for owner reports rather than manufacturer claims.

Parts availability. Burrs wear and stones need dressing. A mill whose parts are discontinued has a limited life.

Warranty. Longer warranties on mills tend to correlate with build quality more than in most appliance categories.

Two things advertised more than they matter: motor wattage on its own, which tells you little without knowing the mechanism, and claims about nutrient preservation, which are difficult to substantiate and not the reason to choose between these.

Buying used

Grain mills appear on second hand listings regularly, usually bought with enthusiasm and used a handful of times, which makes them a reasonable used purchase.

Four checks. Confirm all parts are present, since hoppers and burr sets are model specific and expensive to replace. Run it empty and listen, because a labouring or grinding motor is not worth any discount. Check burr or stone condition if the design allows a look, since worn burrs produce coarse flour no adjustment will fix. And confirm replacement parts are still available for that model, because a discontinued mill with a cracked hopper is scrap.

Impact mills have fewer wearing parts and tend to survive neglect better. Burr mills wear predictably and the burrs are often replaceable. Stone mills are the riskiest used purchase, since dressed stones are a specialist job and worn stones may not be economic to fix.

Ask what was milled in it. A mill that has processed oily seeds against its rating may be clogged in ways that are not visible.

The same market works in your favour later. A hand mill bought to test the habit resells easily when you upgrade, which makes starting cheap genuinely low risk.

Where the mill lives

Same principle as every appliance on this site, and it decides how often milling actually happens.

A mill in a cupboard gets used until the novelty fades. A mill standing ready on a counter gets used indefinitely, because the barrier is one decision rather than four.

Impact mills are the hardest to accommodate, being both bulky and loud. Many people site them in a utility room, a garage, or a pantry, which is fine provided the route is short.

Manual mills clamp or bolt to a surface, so they want a sturdy edge and enough clearance to crank. A flimsy table is genuinely frustrating.

Keep the grain next to the mill. A sealed bucket beside the machine removes the last excuse, and grain stores happily at room temperature in a sealed container.

If nothing can stay out, that is useful information rather than a problem to push through. It argues for a compact manual mill, or for accepting that milling will be a weekend activity rather than a weekday one.

WHAT GOES WRONG

A clogged impact mill. Oily or damp material went in. Check the rating and check the grain is dry.

Warm flour behaving oddly in a dough. Impact milling heats it. Let it cool before mixing.

Coarse, gritty flour from a burr mill. Gap set too wide, or a second pass needed. Both are normal.

Buying electric before proving the habit. The most expensive version of the common mistake.

A manual mill abandoned after three uses. Cranking is real work. That is useful information rather than a failure, and it tells you to buy electric.

Stones clogged with oil. Something oily went through a stone mill. Difficult to recover.

Noise complaints from the household. Impact mills are loud. Mill at a reasonable hour.

DO THIS NOW

  1. Answer one question honestly: do you already bake weekly, or are you finding out whether you will?
  2. If you are finding out, buy a hand crank burr mill and accept that it is work.
  3. If you already bake weekly with shop flour, buy an electric impact mill and skip the intermediate step.
  4. Whichever you buy, check what it is rated for before putting anything oily or damp through it.
