# What to Do with Juice Pulp

Every glass produces a surprising quantity of pulp, and most households bin all of it. That is a shame for two reasons: some of it is genuinely good food, and all of it is fibre you paid for.

It is also worth being realistic. Not all pulp is worth rescuing, and a guide that insists every scrap has a use produces a refrigerator full of tubs nobody opens. This page sorts the useful from the compostable.

## Sort it as it comes out

The single change that makes pulp usable is separating it during juicing rather than after.

Most juicers produce mixed pulp because you feed mixed produce. Mixed carrot, cabbage, celery, and apple pulp is difficult to use, since the flavours pull in different directions and the celery contributes stringiness to everything.

If you intend to keep pulp, juice in blocks. Send all the carrot through, empty the bin, then the rest. It costs one extra bin change and gives you a container of clean carrot pulp rather than a container of general vegetable matter.

If that sounds like too much fuss, it probably is for most mornings. Keep the pulp on the days you want it and compost the rest without guilt.

## The pulp worth keeping

**Carrot.** The most useful by a wide margin. It is sweet, brightly coloured, holds moisture, and disappears into almost anything. It goes into muffins and loaf cakes, fritters and rösti, veggie burgers, meatballs, and any slow cooked sauce that wants bulk. A handful stirred into a bolognese changes nothing about the flavour and adds real substance.

**Beetroot.** Strong colour and an earthy sweetness. Excellent in chocolate cake, where the earthiness disappears and the moisture is welcome. Good in burgers and in a hummus style dip. It will stain everything it touches, including your hands and the container.

**Apple.** Browns fast, so use it the same day. Stir into porridge, fold into a crumble topping, or dry it in a very low oven into a chewy snack. It brings sweetness and a little acidity.

**Ginger.** Tiny quantities, and worth saving. Add to a stir fry, steep in hot water for a rough tea, or freeze in a small bag and add to soups.

**Fennel and celery root.** Both hold enough flavour to be worth folding into a stock or a soup base.

## The pulp to compost

**Celery.** Almost entirely spent fibre after juicing, and unpleasantly stringy. Occasionally worth adding to a stock, more often not.

**Cucumber.** Very little left. Compost it.

**Cabbage and leafy greens.** Fibrous, slightly bitter after extraction, and they do not improve baking. Stock or compost.

**Citrus.** If you peeled the fruit as you should have, what remains is spent membrane. Compost.

**Anything from a mixed batch you cannot identify.** Compost it. Guessing produces bad food.

The [juicing hub](hub-juicing.html) makes the same point about the fibre that juicing sets aside. Composting is a good outcome rather than a failure. Juice pulp breaks down quickly and is a useful green input for a heap carrying too much cardboard or dry leaves.

## Freeze first, decide later

The single habit that makes pulp usable is freezing it on the day rather than deciding on the day.

Refrigerated pulp gives you two or three days to have an idea, and most people do not have one in that window. Frozen pulp gives you two months, which is long enough for a weekend when baking sounds appealing.

Portion it into small bags or an ice cube tray as it comes out of the machine, while you are already at the sink. Label the bag with what it is, because carrot and beetroot are indistinguishable once frozen and produce very different cakes.

Frozen pulp goes straight into batter, sauces, and soups without thawing. For fritters, thaw and squeeze out the extra water first.

## Three things to actually make

**Carrot pulp fritters.** Combine carrot pulp with an egg, a spoonful of flour, a chopped spring onion, and salt. Form into small patties and fry in a hot pan for two minutes a side. The pulp is already dry, which is exactly what a fritter wants, and it holds together better than grated carrot does.

**Beetroot chocolate loaf.** Substitute a cup of beetroot pulp for part of the liquid in any chocolate loaf recipe. The moisture stays, the earthiness vanishes under the cocoa, and the crumb improves.

**Vegetable stock base.** Freeze mixed pulp in a bag alongside your other trimmings. When the bag is full, simmer with an onion, a bay leaf, and peppercorns for forty minutes and strain. This is the lowest effort use and takes any pulp at all, including the kinds listed above as compost candidates.

## Pulp in the garden

If you have any outdoor space at all, the pulp problem largely solves itself, and juice pulp is unusually good compost material.

It is high in nitrogen, already shredded, and holds moisture well, which makes it an excellent green input for a heap that has too much brown material in it. Most home compost heaps are carbon heavy from cardboard, paper, and dry leaves, and juice pulp corrects that balance directly.

Two cautions. Add it in thin layers rather than dumping a whole bin in one place, since a thick wet mass of pulp goes anaerobic and starts to smell. And cover each addition with something dry: shredded paper, dry leaves, or straw. That single step prevents fruit flies almost entirely.

Worm bins take juice pulp very well, and it is close to an ideal food for them. Introduce it gradually and avoid large quantities of citrus, since the acidity is unpopular with worms.

Direct mulching works for the fibrous kinds. Celery and cabbage pulp can go straight onto beds under a layer of mulch, where it breaks down within weeks. Do not leave it exposed on the surface.

If you have no garden, many councils now accept food waste in a collection, and juice pulp qualifies. That is a considerably better outcome than landfill and takes no effort beyond using the right bin.

## Storing it

Pulp is wet plant matter and behaves accordingly.

Refrigerated in a sealed container it holds two or three days. Beyond that it develops an off smell before it looks wrong, so trust your nose rather than the date.

Frozen it holds a couple of months. Freeze in portions rather than one block, since you will never use a whole block at once, and label it clearly. Carrot and beetroot pulp look almost identical once frozen and behave very differently in a cake.

Drying is an option for apple and carrot. Spread thin on a tray in the lowest oven setting for two to three hours until leathery. Dried carrot pulp keeps for weeks and can be added to soups directly.

## Pulp differs by machine

The same produce gives noticeably different pulp depending on what extracted it, and that changes what the pulp is good for.

A masticating machine produces dry, compact pulp, because the auger has pressed most of the liquid out. This is the useful kind. It holds together in fritters, it does not waterlog a cake batter, and it dries quickly in a low oven.

A centrifugal machine produces wetter, fluffier pulp, because the fast blade shreds rather than presses. It works fine in sauces and soups where extra moisture disappears, and it makes poor fritters without being squeezed in a cloth first.

The pulp is also a diagnostic. Squeeze a handful over the sink after any batch. Dry, cohesive pulp means the machine is extracting well. Pulp that drips means juice is going in the bin, and on a masticating machine the fix is usually to feed more slowly.

Twin gear machines produce the driest pulp of all, which is part of what you pay for.

## The honest arithmetic

A typical vegetable-led glass produces roughly as much pulp by weight as it does juice. That is a lot of material arriving daily if you juice every morning.

Nobody uses all of it. Aiming to is how people end up with six tubs in a refrigerator and a sense of failure. A realistic target is keeping the carrot and beetroot pulp two or three times a week, composting the rest, and freezing anything you cannot get to.

The other honest point: pulp is not a substitute for eating fibre generally. It is a way of recovering some of what juicing sets aside, and a good use of something you already bought. Whole vegetables at other meals do that job better.

## Run the numbers

Illustrative figures only, and they will vary with your machine and produce.

| Pulp | Best use | Keeps refrigerated |
|---|---|---|
| Carrot | Fritters, baking, sauces, burgers | 2 to 3 days |
| Beetroot | Chocolate cake, burgers, dips | 2 to 3 days |
| Apple | Porridge, crumble, dried snack | Same day |
| Ginger | Stir fry, tea, soups | Freeze it |
| Celery | Stock, or compost | 2 days |
| Cabbage and greens | Stock, or compost | 2 days |
| Cucumber, citrus | Compost | Compost it now |

A masticating machine produces noticeably drier pulp than a centrifugal one, which both means better yield and makes the pulp more useful in baking. Squeeze a handful over the sink: if liquid runs freely, you are leaving juice behind and the pulp will be too wet for fritters.

## What goes wrong

**Mixed pulp nobody can use.** Juice in blocks and empty the bin between produce types, or accept that the batch is compost.

**Pulp that smelled off after two days.** Normal. Freeze it on day one if you know you will not use it.

**Soggy fritters.** Pulp too wet, which usually means the machine is under extracting. Feed more slowly, or squeeze the pulp in a cloth first.

**Everything stained pink.** Beetroot. There is no fix, only prior warning.

**Six tubs in the refrigerator.** Aim lower. Keeping the carrot pulp twice a week is a win; keeping everything is a project.

## Do this now

1. Tomorrow, send all your carrot through first and empty the bin before the rest. Keep that container.
2. Make fritters with it: one egg, a spoonful of flour, a spring onion, salt, two minutes a side.
3. Start a freezer bag for mixed pulp and trimmings destined for stock.
4. Compost the celery and cucumber pulp without feeling bad about it.
