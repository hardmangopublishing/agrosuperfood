# Storing Dried Food So It Lasts Years

A batch of dried food represents an afternoon of work and a machine running for eight hours. What happens in the following ten minutes determines whether it lasts three months or three years.

Storage is also where the two most common failures in home drying occur, and both are entirely preventable.

!!fig img-drying-variables

## The four enemies, ranked

**Moisture** does the most damage and does it fastest. Any water remaining in the food, or any that gets in afterwards, allows mould and bacterial growth. This is the only one that makes food unsafe rather than merely worse.

**Oxygen** drives oxidation, which fades colour, flattens flavour, and turns any fat content rancid. It is slow and relentless, and it is the main reason a jar that was perfect at month three tastes dull at month twelve.

**Light** degrades colour and some vitamins. Visible over months rather than weeks, and easily eliminated by putting things in a cupboard.

**Heat** accelerates all three. As a rough rule, every ten degrees of storage temperature roughly halves shelf life. A jar stored at fifteen degrees will outlast an identical jar at twenty five by a very large margin.

Address them in that order and the rest follows.

## Conditioning comes first

Nothing about containers matters if the food was not dry enough, and conditioning is the step that establishes whether it was.

Even a well dried batch has variation between pieces, as the [drying hub](hub-drying.html) explains. One slice was slightly thicker, one sat over a weak spot in the airflow. Conditioning equalises the remaining moisture across the whole batch and reveals any piece that was underdried, before that piece spoils a jar you open in February.

Pack the cooled batch loosely into a clear glass jar, about two thirds full. Seal it, leave it somewhere you will look at it daily, and shake it once a day for seven to ten days.

Watch the inside of the glass. Condensation means moisture is still present. Return the whole batch to the machine for another hour or two, then condition again from the start.

No condensation after a week means the batch is genuinely ready for long term storage.

Clear glass is essential here, since the whole point is being able to see the inside surface. This is the one stage where an opaque container is useless.

## Containers

**Glass jars with sealing lids** are the everyday answer. Non porous, no flavour transfer, no staining, and you can see what is inside. Fill with as little headspace as practical, since headspace is a reservoir of oxygen sitting against the food.

**Mylar bags** are the long term answer for bulk quantities. They block light completely, seal against moisture, and take up less space than jars. They need heat sealing and they cannot be reopened and resealed indefinitely, which makes them right for a year's supply and wrong for a jar you dip into weekly.

**Vacuum sealed bags** remove most of the oxygen mechanically. Excellent, with one caveat for dried food: sharp brittle pieces puncture bags easily, so anything with edges wants a rigid container or a bag inside one.

**Plastic tubs** are the weakest option. Most are permeable to some degree over long periods and many hold odours. Fine for the jar you are working through this month, poor for anything beyond that.

Match container size to how you use the food. A large jar gets opened repeatedly, and every opening replaces the headspace with fresh humid air. Several small jars beat one big one for exactly the same reason six ounce jars beat a large bottle for juice.

## Oxygen absorbers

These are the highest value addition for anything you are storing beyond a year, and they are frequently misunderstood.

An oxygen absorber contains iron powder that reacts with oxygen in a sealed container, pulling the concentration down to a fraction of a percent. It does not create a vacuum and it does not remove moisture. It removes oxygen, and nothing else.

{{product:oxygen-absorbers}} come individually vacuum sealed in small packs, which matters more than it sounds. Once the outer pack is opened the absorbers begin working immediately on the air around them, so a large bag opened once is largely spent within an hour. Buying them in small sealed packs of five or ten means you use a pack and reseal nothing.

Sizing is straightforward. A 300cc absorber suits roughly a quart or litre jar. Larger containers want more, and over-sizing is harmless while under-sizing does nothing useful.

Work quickly when you use them. Open the pack, drop one into each jar, seal the jars, and put any remainder into a small airtight container immediately. A jar with an absorber will often pull its lid down slightly over the following day, which is a good sign rather than a fault.

Do not use them with anything that still holds meaningful moisture. Removing oxygen from a damp environment favours the organisms that do not need it, which is the opposite of what you want.

## Desiccants are a different thing

Silica gel packets absorb moisture rather than oxygen, and the two get confused constantly.

For properly dried and conditioned food in a sealed container, a desiccant is unnecessary. The food is already below the moisture threshold and the container keeps new moisture out.

They are genuinely useful for powders, which absorb humidity from the air far faster than slices do, and for anyone in a persistently humid climate opening jars frequently.

Never use a desiccant as a substitute for proper drying. Pulling moisture out of underdried food after the fact is not something a small sachet can do reliably.

## Labelling

Two pieces of information on every container: what it is, and when it was dried.

This sounds trivial and it is the difference between a pantry you use and a shelf of mysteries. Dried apple and dried pear are genuinely indistinguishable at eleven months. So are onion, leek, and celeriac. So are most stone fruits.

The date tells you rotation order, and for dried food it usually indicates declining quality rather than a safety limit. Nothing here becomes dangerous on a date; it becomes less worth eating.

Write on the lid as well as the side, since jars in a cupboard are usually seen from above.

## Realistic shelf life

Stored properly, meaning airtight, dark, cool, and conditioned:

**Fruit.** One year or more. Colour and flavour decline before safety does.

**Vegetables.** One to two years. They outlast fruit because there is less sugar to degrade.

**Herbs.** About a year of good flavour, and considerably longer of safe but dull leaves. Store whole and crush at use.

**Powders.** Six months to a year. They lose aroma much faster than the slices they came from.

**Jerky and dried meat.** One to two months at room temperature, longer refrigerated or frozen. Fat content is the limiting factor and fat goes rancid.

With oxygen absorbers and mylar, fruit and vegetables extend considerably beyond these figures. Without conditioning, none of them apply.

## Rotation, and using what you store

A pantry that only accumulates is a pantry that eventually gets thrown away, and rotation is the habit that prevents it.

**Store in the order you will use.** Newest at the back, oldest at the front, exactly as a shop does. This costs nothing and it is the whole of rotation.

**Keep a working jar and a reserve.** A small jar in the kitchen that you actually dip into, refilled from a larger sealed container elsewhere. This keeps the bulk sealed while making the food genuinely accessible, and accessibility is what decides whether it gets eaten.

**Open the reserve as rarely as possible.** Every opening exchanges the air inside. A container opened once a month ages considerably faster than one opened twice a year.

**Review the shelf every six months.** Anything approaching a year gets moved to the front and used deliberately. This is when soup base jars and mushroom powder earn their keep, since both absorb surplus easily.

**Do not preserve for its own sake.** The purpose is food you eat, not a display of jars. A jar of dried apricots finished in March is a better outcome than the same jar still sealed in September.

## Run the numbers

Illustrative figures. Actual life depends on how thoroughly the food was dried and how cool the storage is.

| Method | Oxygen removed | Light blocked | Reopenable | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glass jar, sealed lid | No | No | Yes | Weekly use |
| Glass jar in a dark cupboard | No | Yes | Yes | The default |
| Glass jar plus oxygen absorber | Yes | No | Once | A year plus |
| Vacuum sealed bag | Mostly | No | No | Bulk, soft items |
| Mylar plus oxygen absorber | Yes | Yes | No | Long term bulk |
| Plastic tub | No | No | Yes | This month only |

The default for most households is glass jars in a dark cupboard, sized to a couple of months of use. Absorbers and mylar are for the surplus you are genuinely storing rather than eating.

## What goes wrong

**Mould weeks or months later.** Underdried, and not conditioned. This is the failure the conditioning jar exists to catch, and it accounts for most spoiled batches.

**Faded colour and flat flavour at six months.** Light and oxygen. Move to a dark cupboard and use absorbers for anything long term.

**A rancid smell.** Fat content oxidising. Common with nuts, seeds, and anything from meat. Store these cool and use them sooner.

**Absorbers that seem to do nothing.** The pack was opened previously and they were spent before use. Buy small individually sealed packs.

**Brittle pieces puncturing a vacuum bag.** Use a rigid container, or bag inside a tub.

**A pantry of unidentifiable jars.** No labels. Write contents and date on the lid.

## Do this now

1. Condition every batch in a clear jar for a week before it goes anywhere near long term storage, shaking daily and watching for condensation.
2. Move your dried food out of any cupboard that gets warm and into the coolest, darkest one you have.
3. Label the lid of every jar with contents and drying date.
4. For anything you want to keep beyond a year, add a 300cc oxygen absorber per litre jar and seal immediately.
